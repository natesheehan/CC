# The pedagogy behind concept cartography

Concept maps were formalized by Joseph Novak and Bob Gowin in the 1970s at Cornell, building on David Ausubel's theory of *meaningful learning*: people learn best by anchoring new information to concepts they already hold rather than memorizing it in isolation.

A concept map makes that anchoring visible. Ideas become nodes, and labeled, directional links spell out exactly how they relate.

## Why concept maps work

- **Externalizes thinking.** Turning a mental model into a visible, editable diagram makes gaps and misconceptions easier to spot, for the learner and anyone assessing their understanding.
- **Encodes relationships, not just facts.** Naming *how* two ideas relate, such as "is a type of", "produces", or "is distinct from", pushes beyond rote recall toward relational understanding.
- **Builds knowledge socially.** Multiple contributors editing the same map and commenting on individual relations mirrors how disciplines build shared understanding: knowledge is negotiated, not just transmitted.
- **Doubles as formative assessment.** A map's structure, including a missing or incorrect relation, reveals a great deal about someone's mental model without requiring a separate test.

Concept Cartography leans into this approach: every concept and relation has its own shareable link, relations have comment threads for discussing a connection, and an activity feed tracks how a map's shared understanding evolves over time.
